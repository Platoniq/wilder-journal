We speak with **Samra Said** about legislative theatre as a practice of co-creation, visualisation and democratic decision-making. Drawing on projects in Belfast, Haringey, Coventry and Medway, Said reflects on what happens before a performance, how people with lived experience shape policy, what it takes to sustain commitments afterwards, and why imagination, trust and playfulness belong in political spaces.

### **When theatre transforms those who take part**

**Olivier: Is there a legislative theatre project, or a particular moment in one, that has stayed with you? It could be a scene, an audience intervention, something someone said afterwards, or a policy change.**

**Samra Said:** I think about the most recent project we did, and what has really stayed with me is the transformation of the actors involved. Some arrived at the first rehearsal very timid, very reserved, not outgoing at all. By the end, they had been completely transformed by their participation, their confidence, their creativity and their artistic output.

One of the actors emailed after a rehearsal and said, “I've written this poem. I've been compiling the lived experiences of everyone in the room. Let me know what you think and whether we can use it in the play.” We did use it, as the final scene. It was really, really powerful. It received such a strong response from the audience and from the other actors, who could recognise their own experiences in different stanzas. There was a real feeling of being held in that space.

It is amazing how, when you bring creative outlets other than theatre into a rehearsal process, magical things can happen. This actor had never written poetry before. They had not realised they had that creative side, and now they have discovered it. That was remarkable to witness, and it was made possible through a legislative theatre process.

The project was called Renters Voice Legislative Theatre, in Belfast, Northern Ireland. We brought together private renters from across Northern Ireland to work on homelessness prevention in the private rented sector. We worked with about nine actors and an audience of around 100 people from Belfast.

**Olivier: The rehearsal seems to be where much of the transformation happens. What do you see taking place in that room that the final audience never sees?**

**Samra Said:** First of all, I never take trust lightly. Trust is always in the room, even if it is not something you can immediately see. We build it through games, playfulness and silliness, through spending time together. The audience sees confident actors on stage, but it doesn't see the trust-building that began in rehearsals: the willingness to share lived experiences, to recognise a common problem and to believe that we might change something together.

The briefing also matters enormously. People sometimes tell us, “What you said would happen when you first briefed us is exactly what happened.” You must be clear from the beginning about the vision, the time commitment and what you are going to provide, whether that is travel, childcare or other support. If anything changes, you have to communicate it properly. People have placed their trust in you.

In this particular process we also provided mental health support. We gave participants a code they could use to call a counsellor or therapist without even having to tell us. We were unpacking a lot of difficult experiences. Some people used that service; others were simply grateful to know it was available. These are things the audience never sees.

And the audience does not necessarily see the transformation of the person who wrote the poem: how their confidence grew, how they began to trust us and to trust that what they said in the room would be taken seriously. That still blows me away. It makes me reflect on our facilitation and what it can enable.

But there is also something the audience doesn't see afterwards. At Arts & Homelessness International, and with the partners we work with, we often say that the next day is the most important day. We don't want to finish a performance and then disappear. We build in a scrutiny group, bringing together actors and the policy team, to follow what happens next. We make space for celebration, but also a pathway for people to stay involved. Ideally, we plan that follow-up from the very beginning, when participants first commit to the rehearsals and performance.

For instance, in Medway, Kent, one of the actors was a freelance creative practitioner with lived experience of homelessness. They are now one of our key producers at Arts & Homelessness International and are doing remarkable work in Medway, including leading the local Arts and Homelessness Forum and working in creative health. Other actors became peer workers. These relationships began in that rehearsal room, through getting to know each other and building trust, but they continued because we made a commitment to keep working together.

We exist to bring positive change to people, projects and policy. And that change includes policymakers. There is a lot of work to do with the policy team around power: how much power do you have, and how do you understand and use it? Sometimes someone will say, “I would love to commit to this, but I need to run it by my team.” Of course there are organisational processes, but in that performance they are also there in their capacity as a director or head of service. They do have power.

I used to assume that senior policymakers would be comfortable with that. Often they're not. They may worry that they will be attacked or that people will expect them to deliver things they cannot deliver because of budget restrictions. We have to see them as human beings who want to be part of the process, too. It is remarkable when they move from scepticism to advocacy.

Housing Rights, for example, has now submitted another funding application to integrate legislative theatre into its policy work. The team has brought the method into its own meetings and discussions. That is a huge legacy. But legacy takes time, energy, commitment, a team and a vision. And so much of it starts in the rehearsal room.

### **From the stage to public policy: sustaining commitments**

**Olivier: How do you carry that rehearsal-room mindset into the scrutiny group and the longer-term work of following up with policymakers? Do you use other creative methodologies there as well?**

**Samra Said:** I think that was missing in some of our earlier scrutiny meetings. Creativity is one of our organisational values, so we would always begin with a creative icebreaker, but we weren't necessarily thinking of the whole scrutiny meeting as a creative space.

Over the past two years I've been experimenting more with that. How do you make a scrutiny meeting interesting? How do you bring images into it? How do you create a moment to celebrate when a policy commitment moves forward? Could you use image theatre or another creative method to mark that progress? And how do you open up space for other people to facilitate, rather than everything falling on us?

It should feel different from a conventional council or committee meeting, where information is delivered by one side to the other. We want to bring the actors and policy team into a more equal relationship. Otherwise the conversation can become, “Where are you with proposal number one?” A policy officer explains why it hasn't happened, perhaps because of barriers or resources, and the energy in the room disappears.

How do we shift that conversation? This is a collective project. The proposals came from citizens, the audience and the actors. We are all responsible for moving them from pending to achieved.

**Olivier: Once recommendations reach the desk of a policymaker, they can become institutionalised and lose the energy and meaning of the process that produced them. People who co-created them may also lose track of what happened. How do you avoid that?**

**Samra Said:** Katie Rubin has taught us a lot about the importance of tracking commitments. We establish a policy tracker, share it with partners and decide early on who is responsible for following it up.

In our latest Belfast project, Arts & Homelessness International supports that work, but Housing Rights takes the lead. Kerry Logan, who co-facilitated the process, is a brilliant example of why that matters. She originally commissioned a project with us. Then, as part of its legacy, we arranged training with Katie Rubin for around 20 practitioners in Belfast, including Kerry. She went from commissioning a project to coordinating it and then co-facilitating it.

That is exactly the kind of change we want. We don't want to be the only people who can do this. How can we make sure other people have the resources, knowledge, insight and, importantly, the opportunity to practise? In the latest process we co-facilitated, co-designed and co-directed everything together. It was so much fun.

**Olivier: We have also worked with Katie on digitalising the follow-up, including an online policy tracker and legislative theatre tarot cards that invite policymakers to imagine the future adoption of recommendations. It is another way to keep those commitments visible.**

**Samra Said:** I would love to see those cards! That sounds really, really fun.

### **Listening to lived experiences of homelessness to change policy**

**Olivier: Let's turn to homelessness, which is central to your work. What makes legislative theatre particularly powerful in this field? I'm also interested in the relationship to space, although I know that comparing a rehearsal room with a temporary home could be problematic.**

**Samra Said:** Yes, I would be very careful about making an analogy between a rehearsal space and a home, even a temporary one. Homelessness is such a sensitive subject.

For us, the important point is that we advocate for the arts to be part of homelessness services and policy, and for the arts to enable more participatory democracy in decision-making. Historically, housing and homelessness strategies have often been developed through consultation. A draft document goes out to citizens, who are invited to have their say, but the strategy has not necessarily been co-created from start to finish. It is still often a head of homelessness or a policy adviser who goes away and writes it.

If you're serious about co-creation, you need to use art and creative methods to engage people affected by the issue. You also need to change a relationship that can be hostile or marked by misunderstanding between councils and the people experiencing the consequences of their decisions. There are good reasons why that mistrust exists.

I used to coordinate the Greater Manchester Homelessness Action Network. During that time, Katie Rubin introduced legislative theatre in Greater Manchester. I wasn't working for Arts & Homelessness International then, but I saw how powerful it could be for a homelessness prevention strategy. We worked on three issues identified with a large group of people, and the recommendations contributed to Greater Manchester's homelessness prevention strategy. To this day, people at Greater Manchester Combined Authority have continued to use creative methodologies and support practitioner training.

That experience helped us see a real connection between legislative theatre and our mission. We then approached Haringey Council in London, whose staff at the time were doing some quite radical and values-led work, about the possibility of a legislative theatre process.

But we don't start by assuming we have all the answers. We begin with a mapping exercise. When a council commissions us, we map the homelessness and arts services that already exist in the area. We visit hostels, bed-and-breakfast accommodation and places where people affected by homelessness access services. We talk with people and introduce ourselves. Some of them may later take part as actors, so that is already the beginning of trust-building. We're showing up, listening and making clear that what people contribute will be taken seriously.

It is a form of consultation and co-production, but not full co-creation yet. We are hearing experiences, building relationships and creating momentum before starting the legislative theatre process itself.

People tell us about problems with temporary accommodation or rough sleeping. Someone might say they weren't verified by an outreach team because the workers came between two and four, while they were somewhere else because they did not feel safe. All these testimonies and lived experiences contain knowledge that can lead to policy change and support other people across the borough.

The Haringey process fed into the council's homelessness and rough sleeping strategy. Some proposals focused on practical changes: better information about the support available to staff and people experiencing homelessness, stronger resources for the homelessness hub, more effective triage and same-day decisions in urgent cases, and a more welcoming service with staff and refreshments. One commitment involved exploring changes with Mulberry Junction, including how the services available at the hub were communicated.

I remember that project particularly well because we were coming out of the Covid lockdowns. We had recruited actors, and then another wave hit just as rehearsals were about to begin. People were understandably worried about leaving their accommodation. We had to recruit again only days before the first rehearsal. We were all wearing masks and trying to respond to changing guidance. It was very challenging. But you could also see how the pandemic was intensifying everything people were experiencing. They were deeply committed because they wanted change.

**Olivier: You've described mapping, diagnosis and testimony before the theatrical process begins. What can legislative theatre make visible that testimony alone cannot? Does putting a system into bodies, relationships and space produce a different kind of understanding?**

**Samra Said:** For me, one of the differences is that the process commits us to actual policy change. We document proposals, make commitments to actors and audiences, and work to ensure that the origin of a recommendation is visible in the strategy.

In Haringey, even the strategy itself had an artistic dimension. We worked with an artist with lived experience of homelessness who engaged people in the park, asking what Haringey meant to them and exploring the proposals. Their visual work and animation fed into the co-created strategy.

Of course, we don't claim that every part of the strategy came from legislative theatre. But where a proposal did emerge from that process, it needs to be identified and documented. Then the housing and homelessness team can connect it to an implementation plan and funding.

Rather than asking citizens to respond to an email or a document sitting on a website, you bring actors, the public and policymakers into the same room. There is energy, momentum and fun. We are really serious about fun! We believe that everyone is a creative policymaker. People are not just being consulted; they are contributing.

And we make commitments back to the audience: we will send you the report by a particular date; we will share the video; we will let you know what happens next. These become an archive of a real process, including changes that have actually taken place.

It shows councils and other institutions that there are alternatives to conventional consultation and co-production. You can make the process more engaging, more creative and more enjoyable, but it requires funding. Above all, it requires time. We need to know, from the beginning, who is going to take the commitment forward, who is answerable for it and who will follow up with the policy team.

In Haringey and Medway, scrutiny panels continued for around a year and a half. In Medway, one of the strongest legacies was handing over the Arts and Homelessness Forum to the community, who now lead it with the council. They are doing phenomenal work, including developing a memorial for people affected by homelessness. That is the kind of legacy that matters.

### **Making data, barriers and power relations visible**

**Olivier: Could we understand legislative theatre as a form of visualisation? In our research, we're interested in visual practices that make barriers, power relations and systems visible, not only in conventional data visualisation. How do you bring data into a theatre process?**

**Samra Said:** We don't shy away from data at all. Between rehearsals we have moments of research. We ask the actors: these are the issues you're identifying; what do we need to know about them? What does the legislation say? What evidence do we have?

Something distinctive about several of our processes is that the actors are roughly half people with lived experience of homelessness and half council or housing staff. So the knowledge and the data are also in the room. Staff can explain how a policy works or take responsibility for finding out more and coming back at the next rehearsal.

We use data to strengthen our understanding of the issue. And we often say to a council: it doesn't matter if you tell us that a service already exists if the people who are supposed to access it don't know about it or aren't accessing it. Something isn't working. So how can we change that, or co-create a different version with the people who need it?

In Medway, I remember a scene using a big book of legislation as an image. We wanted to make the weight of the law visible in the scene itself. In Belfast, we also used percentages and information about the private rented sector. There were slides displaying current data as the audience entered, and we installed visual artworks made during rehearsals throughout the venue. Those artistic outputs also appeared in the report.

There is a world in which numbers, research, lived experience and artistic expression can be combined. We want people who connect with one form of knowledge to encounter the others.

**Olivier: What about visualising a future in which the recommendations have actually been implemented? Could participants themselves imagine what the data, or the world it describes, would look like after change?**

**Samra Said:** Absolutely. There is something important about bringing imagination into the process. In the most recent project, once we had finalised three proposals from the audience, I asked everyone — audience members, actors and the policy team — to make an image through image theatre of what the future would look like if all three had been achieved. It was really powerful.

You could see different responses. Some people were aligned, others were very optimistic, some were hopeful, and others were connecting to the possibilities in different ways. It made the future something we could collectively picture.

We also put actual information into the scenes. In Belfast, for example, an actor described what landlords currently require in the private rented sector. The scene made visible why some of those requirements were unrealistic and why people wanted them changed. Landlords, housing professionals and people from the Northern Ireland Housing Executive in the audience could recognise which policies or regulations were relevant.

When we asked people to turn to their neighbours and discuss possible solutions, you could hear the knowledge in the room. People drew on their professional work and experience, identifying what already existed, where the limits were and what the barriers might be.

There was also dissent. Someone worried that if all the proposed changes were made, landlords might stop renting out their properties. That fear became part of the discussion. We have to make space for those concerns while still insisting that we can imagine a fairer private rented sector. We are human beings, and we should be able to live in a fair and equitable society.

I think we could do much more to integrate different kinds of knowledge from the very beginning. For example, who are the data analysts or the people working on imaginative approaches inside the council? Could they be part of the creative process earlier, rather than us taking all the research on ourselves? That is a big takeaway for me.

### **Poetry, music and other ways of imagining change**

**Olivier: And perhaps participants could choose their own language for interpreting or expressing that information — dance, poetry, spoken word or other forms beyond legislative theatre. Have you brought those artistic practices into your projects?**

**Samra Said:** Yes. In Haringey, one of the actors was a singer and began the play with a song. We hadn't planned it in advance. It came out of rehearsal, and we realised how powerful and relevant it was to what we were exploring. Unfortunately we didn't film the whole performance, because we were still working under Covid conditions, but I remember that opening very clearly.

We've also used illustration, animation and visual art. People have made paintings in response to what emerged in the rehearsal room. Kerry, who was involved in the Belfast processes in consecutive years, created a painting each time. It was visually very powerful, but it was also important for her to be able to put that energy into an artistic form that was her own.

We haven't really incorporated choreography yet. I have been going to lots of musicals recently and watching them thinking, “We're missing something here!” Maybe that's something we could invest in for future legislative theatre work. Watch this space.

But poetry, spoken word, singing, illustration and visual art have definitely all become part of different performances and processes we've created.

**Olivier: Theatre is often imagined as requiring a stage, with the policymakers brought into the spotlight. Have you tried working in spaces that aren't theatres? Does the venue change what becomes possible?**

**Samra Said:** We haven't used private homes, but we have used community halls, libraries and other places people already know.

In Coventry, for instance, we worked in community spaces, including halls used for outreach and local services. Some are places of worship at weekends and places where people find services, activities or support during the week. We also organised creative sessions around the housing and homelessness strategy, inviting people to respond to its main themes: what is missing, what is strong, what needs to change?

The turnout was strong, partly because people knew and trusted the space. They knew where to get a cup of tea. They knew where things were and how to move around. They didn't feel out of place. That familiarity is really important to how you welcome and care for people.

In Haringey, we staged the legislative theatre performance in a library. You walked in surrounded by books, then entered a hall within the library. It was a place local people already accessed for the internet, free Wi-Fi and warmth, especially because we were doing the project in winter. Again, people knew how to get there and felt comfortable in the building.

In Belfast we used The MAC, a cultural and artistic venue, and that worked for the people we wanted to reach, too. The point is not that one kind of venue is always better. Choosing the space is an intentional part of designing the process. We think about the actors and the communities we want to involve, and about where people will feel welcome.

### **What if Parliament learned to play?**

**Olivier: In democratic innovation, citizens are often invited into institutional settings and asked to act like policymakers — citizens' assemblies are an obvious example. Even climate assemblies rarely happen in a forest. Imagine you had the budget to transform the way politicians deliberate in Parliament for one day. Instead of their usual discursive methods, perhaps they would have to sing or make theatre. How would you organise that day?**

**Samra Said:** That's really interesting. I think what is often missing in those spaces is play. People are suited and booted, and sometimes completely disconnected from the realities on the ground.

I would begin by facilitating some playfulness. There would also need to be responsibility. People should understand who they are in the process and what power or responsibility they hold. Maybe their positions don't have to change, but the way they relate to one another could.

We could create serious fun. We are here to make serious recommendations and serious proposals, but fun can be at the heart of that. And I wouldn't want it to be only politicians. Citizens need to be in the room; citizen power needs to be in the room. Then we can create something collectively.

Of course, there are questions of access and barriers, and I know I'm talking about an ideal world. But playfulness would be the starting point. There is room for it in Parliament, in politics and wherever power is exercised. People need to stay humble and not lose sight of their power to make change. We've seen change happen before; it is possible. But imagination requires time, funding, energy and commitment.

**Olivier: Suppose you could begin on Monday with an enormous budget. You could bring back all the groups you have worked with. What would you do?**

**Samra Said:** I would do a choir! A really strong choir.

My colleague Matt is a wonderful singer, and we have other colleagues who are musicians. I would bring them together with the groups we've worked with in Haringey, Coventry and Belfast, all in one room with policymakers.

Maybe it would also be a moment to recognise the policymakers who have been part of those processes, to give them the opportunity to share what they have been working on and the commitments they have made. We could take the advocacy for legislative theatre beyond the local and regional work we've been doing.

The choir's lyrics could speak to the issues and the commitments that have emerged from all those projects. Then we would invite policymakers and everyone else in the room to make a commitment to them. That would be quite something.
