What does it mean to speak or be silenced when the digital sphere becomes one of the main battlegrounds of the Palestinian struggle?


In this interview with **7amleh (The Arab Center for the Advancement of Social Media)**, we explore how Palestinian narratives are shaped, restricted, erased and defended in today's platform-dominated public sphere. From the structural biases embedded in content moderation systems to the political pressure shaping the decisions of Meta, Google and other global actors, 7amleh guides us through the mechanisms of digital repression: the over-moderation of Arabic, the tolerance of incitement in Hebrew, the illusion of technological neutrality and the deep power imbalances that define Palestinian visibility online.


### **Could you explain what 7amleh is?**


**7amleh:** 7amleh is a Palestinian organisation working to protect digital rights. In Arabic, “hamleh” means “campaign”. The number 7 is used because it represents the Arabic letter “ḥ” when writing in the Latin alphabet.


### **How would you describe the current situation of Palestinian voices in the digital space?**


We would say that they remain largely marginalised. Sometimes it is difficult to convince people that digital rights are human rights. Some people, especially now with the genocide in Gaza, say that this is not a priority. But we argue that it is, because without digital rights there are no telecommunications: you cannot call an ambulance or share your narrative on social media. More and more people are speaking out, both Palestinians and supporters of Palestine, especially after 7 October and the censorship that followed.


{% quote %}In general, the policies decided within Meta or Google go against Palestinian interests {% endquote %}


Many people who were previously unaware of what was happening in Palestine began to notice online censorship on platforms like Instagram and the way traditional media spoke about Palestinians. That is how Palestinian voices began to be heard more widely.


### **Do you think there is algorithmic censorship targeting these communities?**


Yes, there is a paradox. Digital platforms are not neutral: they are for-profit companies. They protect their own interests and do not necessarily promote content that benefits society. At the same time, they are opportunistic: if something starts trending, they promote it, as we saw with TikTok. But in general, the policies decided within Meta or Google go against Palestinian interests. There is a clear link between these platforms and far-right ideologies. They have collaborated with the Israeli government and Trump, promoting narratives that harm Palestinians.


### **Many platforms claim to be neutral, but 7amleh argues that this neutrality is an illusion. Can you give concrete examples of how this lack of neutrality affects Palestinians?**


For example, we know that Meta has over-moderated Arabic content. They use what they call a *classifier*, a database of words used to moderate a language, and the Arabic classifier is overloaded. So many words are flagged as suspicious that it reflects a racist assumption that Arabic is dangerous or linked to terrorism.


One example is the word *shahid*, which means “martyr”. Meta treated it as a sign of support for terrorism, but in Arabic and in the Palestinian context, it simply means someone who has died. It could even refer to a child killed in Gaza. Meta was unwilling to understand these cultural differences.


Meanwhile, moderation in Hebrew is minimal because Meta considers Hebrew a “small language” and not a strategic priority, even though Danish, which is also a small language, has a full classifier. In conflict zones such as Israel and Palestine, Hebrew should be properly moderated because there is incitement and harmful content. In our latest report on Meta, we showed that the company allowed hate speech and incitement on Instagram and Facebook, including posts by Israeli leaders calling for the ethnic cleansing of Palestinians or sexual violence against women in Gaza. The same happens with Google and YouTube: they allow this type of content in Hebrew while over-moderating videos in Arabic.


When they tell us these are “algorithmic errors”, we do not accept that explanation. If they make mistakes, they need to do better. We are talking about Facebook, Instagram, Meta, Google… these companies are wealthier than entire countries, wealthier than continents. They can invest money in improving their moderation systems.

### **7amleh has developed *The Digital Record* as a community-based tool to document digital representation and make it more visible. How did this initiative come about, and what are its main objectives in relation to the community?**


If NGOs do not do this work, nobody will. It is one thing to say that platforms are not neutral, and quite another to prove it. To take legal action or produce reports, we need evidence, and we cannot obtain it without the support of communities.


We want to empower users to understand how platforms shape their narratives and public image, and give them the tools to defend themselves. We ask them to share their experiences because each individual case is part of a pattern of systemic discrimination.


We offer free support if their content is removed, their accounts are suspended or they experience *shadow banning*. We help them submit complaints and challenge censorship. In return, they provide us with evidence: examples of account suspensions, words that trigger penalties, journalists being censored and unmoderated incitement in Hebrew. Some of our most valuable evidence comes from employees of the technology companies themselves, who show us internal communications demonstrating that these decisions are deliberate.


### **How can this documentation be used to influence policy or raise awareness?**


For example, in the European Union we have the *Digital Services Act* (DSA), a new regulation governing large platforms. It includes mechanisms for submitting complaints when platforms make discriminatory or incorrect moderation decisions. The evidence we collect allows us to submit complaints to the European Commission. Without evidence, we could not do this, which is why documentation is essential.


### **7amleh is in contact with EU institutions and policymakers. What kind of relationship do you have with them?**


We meet with them three or four times a year. We meet with DigiConnect, the European Commission office responsible for the DSA, and with DigiMENA, which focuses on the MENA region and Palestine. We also work with the EEAS, the European External Action Service, which deals with information manipulation and foreign influence. They tend to focus on Russia or China, but we tell them: look at Israel, because it is also shaping narratives within the EU.


{% quote %}Without narratives, we cannot win. Storytelling allows us to remember people's humanity, something that is sometimes forgotten{% endquote %}


It is not easy to work with them: we do not always see results, but we share our reports and sometimes achieve small changes, although it takes time.


### **Do you think European institutions are willing to address the digital repression experienced by Palestinians?**


When opinions come directly from Palestine, we do not think they care very much. When we talk to them about censorship in Palestine, they respond that it is not their problem. But when we tell them that Israel is paying for advertisements targeting European citizens, they start paying attention, because they recognise that Israel is using the same strategies as Russia or China, and this is perceived as a threat to European democracy.


### **7amleh highlights the importance of narratives in resisting digital repression. How can storytelling become a tool for collective power?**


Without narratives, we cannot win. Storytelling allows us to remember people's humanity, something that is sometimes forgotten. It also gives us control over what is said: instead of others telling our stories, we tell them ourselves.


Social media is essential. That is why we do not call for a boycott: we do not have sustainable alternatives to platforms like Instagram. What matters is how we use them. If we communicate effectively, we can achieve a great deal. After 7 October, many people changed their views on Palestine because of what they saw on social media, as traditional media were not showing the reality.


A good example, although outside Palestine, is the new mayor of New York, Mamdani. He did not win because of Instagram alone, but his messages about the housing crisis and reconnecting with communities were so powerful on social media that even people outside New York started talking about him. If we use platforms effectively, they can benefit us, although they are complex tools.


### **In this context, and speaking of narratives, what lessons can be learned from Palestinian communities facing political transformations?**


We do not want to use the word “resilience”, because Palestinians are tired of being called resilient. They do not want to be resilient: they want to be free and live like anyone else. That is the least they deserve.


We have learned that, even in the midst of genocide, war and death, it is still possible to raise your voice and share a message. If that message is grounded in values, ethics and human rights, people will listen, even if it takes time.


Without Palestinians sharing what is happening in their homeland, public opinion would not have changed. We would probably not be talking about Sudan or Myanmar.


Thanks to them, we are seeing political and cultural changes towards human rights and progressive values, although the far right is also growing. It is a balance, but without them we would not have such politically engaged young people or people willing to challenge their governments. That is very powerful, especially after COVID and years of political apathy.


### **What can an ordinary citizen do to support or take part in this process of digital resistance and solidarity?**


Share accurate information. Follow reliable journalists and media outlets. Post on social media, even if it is something small. We know that many people are afraid of losing their jobs or being judged, but even a simple post, even if it is not political, can make a difference.


Speak out. One person can influence another. Support grassroots organisations and get involved in your neighbourhood or community. There can be no political change without the support of communities. Sometimes we think one person is not enough, but one person can change another person's opinion. Sharing accurate information and following platforms that support digital action for Palestine is key.

## ***Conclusions***


A simple but unsettling truth emerges from this conversation with 7amleh: digital rights are not an optional part of the political struggle, but lie at its very heart. In the Palestinian context, the digital sphere is not just a space for expression, but a fragile lifeline where documentation, memory and survival depend on infrastructures designed in distant places and governed by opaque and often hostile decisions.


The conversation makes it clear that what is at stake is not abstract. Content moderation systems, language classifiers, platform policies, advertising targeting and algorithmic invisibility become instruments that can amplify a community's voice or erase it. In Palestine, these technical decisions determine who is heard, who is vilified and who becomes invisible at a time when visibility means protection.


This resonates deeply with the Platoniq Foundation's commitment to building safer digital spaces. Not “safe” in the sense of isolation or neutrality, something platforms rarely provide, but safer in the sense of being consciously designed to reduce harm, centre marginalised voices and make visible the power dynamics that shape participation. A safe(r) space is not simply a digital room. It is a political commitment: to transparency, accountability, community-led governance and the protection of those most vulnerable to erasure.
