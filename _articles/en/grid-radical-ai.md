*An assembly has been debating for two hours. An AI-generated summary appears on the screen: tidy, clear, reasonable. Almost everyone nods. Only one person raises a hand to say that their objection is no longer there. Nobody rejected it. The synthesis simply did not need it.*

## **Hypothesis**

The democratic challenge of artificial intelligence is not only about making participation more efficient. It is about deciding what roles we want it to play, what knowledge it speaks from, and how it can complement, without replacing, the voices, judgement and responsibility of the people who deliberate.

GRID was born from that question. It explores whether a situated, polyphonic AI can expand our capacity to question and deliberate when we use it collectively, in a real debate. It does not set out to resolve disagreement. It wants to make disagreement more visible, more open to discussion and easier to live with.

## **Three key questions**

Where does an AI speak from when it seems to speak from "nowhere"? And what happens if, instead of hiding that position, we make it explicit?

## **Story of me: why do we think this matters?**

AI is already part of the infrastructure of democratic participation. The OECD identifies [nine families of applications](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/artificial-intelligence-and-the-future-of-citizen-participation_a1ee2e0a-en.html), from translation and transcription to facilitation, simulation and process design. But it insists on one condition: their adoption must protect democratic values, transparency, civic space and citizen participation in the governance of these systems.

From within deliberative theory, Rountree and Gastil sound a similar warning. AI can serve as a tool for *[deliberation-making](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/1625/)*, but it should not become a decision-making tool or replace the encounter between people. Simulating human deliberation in order to skip it would be, in their words, an antidemocratic shortcut.

GRID deliberately places itself at that boundary. It does not want to decide how a conversation ends. It wants to study how a conversation changes when a particular machine is brought into it. The question is not only whether AI can help us deliberate, but whether we can turn its mediation into something we deliberate about.

Research by [Jäske and colleagues](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/02633957261466242) on Finland's Citizens' Assembly on Energy offers an important clue. The same model proved useful for organising and summarising materials, but lost detail and much of its critical edge when it had to draft questions for experts. The lesson is clear: there is no point in evaluating "AI" in the abstract. We have to look at what role it plays, which deliberative values are at stake and what effects it has at each stage of the process.

For GRID, this becomes an experimental protocol. Rather than asking whether an agent gives a "good" answer, we want to observe whether different agents retrieve genuinely different knowledge and arguments, which questions, conflicts or positions vanish when the system summarises and synthesises, and how participants' interpretations shift. We also want to know at what point assistance turns into substitution, and whether a person can identify, question and change the corpus, the instructions and the mediation choices that shaped an answer.

That last point is decisive. Small *prompting* decisions can flatten the complexity and critical force of a contribution. That is why, for GRID, transparency is not a technical requirement. It is a political condition.

## **Against the Habermas Machine: don't automate consensus**

GRID takes a deliberately different position from the [Habermas Machine](https://habermasmachine.ai/), the experiment [developed by Google DeepMind](https://github.com/google-deepmind/habermas_machine). Not because finding common ground is worthless, but because we want to ask what happens when agreement becomes the very thing a machine is built to optimise.

The Habermas Machine takes a group's positions and produces successive collective statements, keeping those that win the most support. It deserves credit for one thing: according to [the study published in *Science*](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adq2852), its statements did not simply flatter the majority. They incorporated dissenting voices and even gave minority critiques more weight than they had in the group. So the problem is not that it ignores the minority. It is that disagreement enters the text as one more ingredient in a statement designed to be approved. GRID starts from the opposite hypothesis. We do not want a machine that finds the best wording for consensus, but one that lets us see what happens to disagreement when an AI steps in.

The difference also shifts where authority lies. In the Habermas Machine, the AI proposes an increasingly acceptable collective statement. In GRID, any synthesis must be open to interruption, discussion, rejection or rewriting by the group. The AI does not decide when there is enough agreement or which disagreements can be dropped. Those remain political questions.

The Habermas Machine asks: **"What statement can bring us together?"**

GRID asks: **"What is this machine doing to produce that statement, what does it leave out, and who gets to question it?"**

Read our [interview with José Luis Martí about this experiment](https://journal.platoniq.net/en/wilder-journal-3/interviews/ia-deliberacion-limites/).

## **Story of us: what we already know**

The question GRID raises is no longer speculative. The report *[Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Citizen Participation](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/artificial-intelligence-and-the-future-of-citizen-participation_a1ee2e0a-en.html)*, published by the OECD with the Bertelsmann Stiftung, analyses fifty cases of AI use in participatory processes across twenty-two countries. From them it identifies nine broad types of application: information production, *sense-making*, translation, transcription, virtual assistance, moderation, facilitation, simulation and the design of participatory processes.

Some of these uses are already widespread: interpreting and organising large volumes of citizen input, supporting participants through conversational interfaces, or lowering language barriers through translation. Deliberative processes are also one of the main testing grounds. The debate is no longer whether AI will mediate democratic processes, but which roles we want to hand over to it and on what terms.

Some experiments push the question further: not what an AI can do, but where it speaks from. In Spain, Afroféminas has developed some particularly thought-provoking projects. Its [Socratic GPT](https://afrofeminas.com/category/afrofeminasgpt/) does not simply answer. It asks questions back and turns the conversation into an exercise in critical thinking. Salud Negra IA builds an agent around a specific field, the health of Black women, from an explicitly antiracist and Afrofeminist standpoint.

The potential benefits are real: lower costs, greater accessibility, the ability to synthesise thousands of contributions or to sustain processes that would otherwise be unmanageable. But the same literature draws a fundamental political line: AI cannot replace the political will to take citizens' perspectives seriously, nor democratic decision-making, nor human responsibility.

[Nor should artificial personas be used as substitutes for the participation of vulnerable or marginalised communities](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/1625/). A simulation is still a construct of the model, and turning it into political representation can deepen the very absence it was meant to fix. Describing a group is not the same as representing it democratically.

That is why, in GRID, the Joker does not represent "the oppressed", the Philosopher does not represent "philosophers" and the Deliberator does not represent "reasonable citizens". They are not synthetic people standing in for absent groups. They are methodological and epistemological positions: different ways of asking questions, reading a situation and shaping an intervention.

## **Story of now: how is the Foundation responding?**

### **A polyphonic, situated architecture**

GRID's architecture starts from a concrete question: what happens if, instead of asking a single AI to adopt the supposedly neutral voice of "the facilitator", we keep within the system several human ways of looking at, questioning and accompanying a deliberation?

Behind GRID's personalities there is more than *prompts*. There are specific people, practices and communities. The Philosopher is built in dialogue with Cristian Palazzi, head of advocacy at Platoniq and a PhD in Philosophy. The Joker draws on lessons from Katy Rubin, a student of Augusto Boal and an expert in Legislative Theatre, and from Olivier Schulbaum, Platoniq's strategic director. The Deliberator grows out of the collective work of the partners in the European project INSPIRE.

This does not mean creating digital avatars of these people or speaking on their behalf. It means recognising that each personality has a human lineage: someone asked these questions before, developed certain practices and decided what it means to facilitate, interpret or intervene. A situated architecture should be able to answer the question: who is behind the way this machine hands the problem back to me?

The **Joker** is the most practical, provocative and theatrical personality. It sees problems as power relations that can be made visible, staged and transformed through collective action. It does not stop at explaining a situation: it asks which scene would show it, who can act, which rules or institutions hold it in place and what could be rehearsed differently. Its job is to unsettle consensus that comes too easily and to bring in democratic imagination.

The **Philosopher** is the conceptual and interpretive personality. Before accepting a question as it arrives, it examines its assumptions: what idea of democracy it holds, what it means by power, what knowledge it treats as valid, what subject it imagines and which voices it leaves out. It works with genealogy, critical theory, postcolonial and decolonial thought, feminisms and situated epistemologies. It is in no hurry to close a problem. It wants to sharpen it and show what an obvious answer might be hiding.

The **Deliberator** has a deliberately collective lineage. It draws on what INSPIRE's organisations, facilitators, participants and partners have learned together. Its concern is how to organise a democratic conversation so that plurality becomes collective thought and action: who takes part and who is missing, how the floor is shared, what information the group needs, what barriers exist and how the deliberation will connect to real decisions.

On top of these three personalities sits a layer of **self-awareness**. It is not a fourth voice on the same level, but a cross-cutting critical vigilance that also looks inward: why is GRID looking at the problem this way? It forces the system to acknowledge which knowledge it privileges, which power relations it may normalise, which experience it turns into "data", which conflict it smooths over and which voices are still missing.

Making the people behind the personalities visible does not remove bias. It makes bias traceable and open to challenge. GRID's polyphony does not claim that by adding enough perspectives we will reach a complete view. It aims for something more modest and perhaps more useful: keeping several positions in sight, showing where they come from and letting participants choose them, compare them, question them or decide that none of them is enough.

### **Against prophetic culture: GRID and schizoanalysis**

Why insist on several personalities instead of building a single, more complete voice? The answer has to do with how we think about the future.

There is a culture we might call prophetic: one that tries to tame the present by speaking about the future from a single point of view. The expert forecast, the trend report, the assistant that answers any question with total assurance. Its promise is to do away with uncertainty. But that promise is a fallacy. A future announced by a single voice is not anticipated; it is imposed. Whoever speaks about tomorrow from a single centre is in fact arranging the present to suit themselves. It is a form of the will to domination.

The philosopher Carissa Véliz makes this case in *[Prophecy: Prediction, Power, and the Fight for the Future, from Ancient Oracles to AI](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/759692/prophecy-by-carissa-veliz/)* (2026). From the ancient oracle to the AI systems that now decide who gets a loan, a job or a home, predictions are rarely neutral descriptions of what is to come. They work as instruments of authority: they shape the future they claim to foresee, blur accountability and take away people's capacity to decide. For Véliz, what prediction really promises is not knowledge of the future but power over others. That is why she proposes the opposite of prophecy: making room for uncertainty and creativity.

Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari gave us precise tools for thinking about this operation. In *Anti-Oedipus* (1972) they called schizoanalysis a practice that refuses to reduce the multiplicity of desire to a single interpretive code, the Oedipal triangle of psychoanalysis, and that maps flows, connections and blockages instead of interpreting them. In *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980) they described this mode of interpretation as a despotic regime of the signifier: every sign refers back to a centre, and a caste of interpreters administers its meaning. It looks a great deal like the position occupied today by a language model that answers with a single voice from "nowhere".

Against that regime they proposed the rhizome: a system without a centre, in which any point can connect to any other, made of multiplicities rather than units, and which produces open maps rather than tracings of what is already known. GRID tries to work as a map, not a tracing. The Joker, the Philosopher and the Deliberator are not pieces of a truth that would add up to the complete answer. They are different ways into the same problem, which can connect, contradict each other or be left suspended. It is worth being precise here: the "schizo" in Deleuze and Guattari is not a clinical figure or a celebration of illness, but the name of a process that undoes single codes. GRID's multiple personalities do not split a subject apart; they stop any one voice from taking ownership of the problem.

That is why GRID is, by design, a paradoxical machine. In *The Logic of Sense* (1969), Deleuze sets paradox against good sense, which imposes a single direction, and against common sense, which fixes identities. Paradox affirms both directions at once. GRID does not resolve the tension between its voices. It holds it, because that is where something appears that none of them can see on their own.

In this framework, uncertainty is not a flaw to be corrected but the very condition of anticipation. In *Difference and Repetition* (1968), Deleuze distinguishes the possible, a future that resembles the present because it is only its copy projected forward, from the virtual, which is actualised only by creating difference. Prophecy works with the possible: it announces a tomorrow it believes it already knows. Anticipation, by contrast, requires keeping open what we do not yet know, and that is why it needs several perspectives at once.

This is where interdependence comes in. For Deleuze and Guattari, whoever speaks is never an isolated subject but a collective assemblage of enunciation: people, languages, institutions and techniques that together produce a statement. GRID makes this explicit. Every answer is the product of a corpus, of human lineages, of retrieval rules and of the group asking the question. By always offering several points of view, GRID does not hand the future over to the machine. It obliges participants to compose it together. Guattari called the idea that producing subjectivity, and with it futures, is above all an act of creation the ethico-aesthetic paradigm (*Chaosmosis*, 1992). That is GRID's wager: collective creativity, not prediction, as the ground for thinking about what comes next.

### **Building the corpus**

Much of the conversation about AI and participation revolves around how to use the technology to scale up, cut costs, synthesise input or speed up collective processes. GRID asks a different question: what is an AI fed on, and how does that diet change what it can see and say?

The question is not a minor one. The knowledge used to train large models has become a commodity: there are companies dedicated to selling clean datasets, and [some critical voices](https://www.eldiario.es/opinion/zona-critica/millones-libros-desguazados-alimentar-ia_129_13461727.html) report that Anthropic went as far as scanning and destroying millions of books to train its model. Against that extractive logic, GRID tries the opposite path: a small corpus, carefully chosen, with names attached.

GRID's corpus starts from an offline library of PDF books gathered over more than twenty years. It currently holds 6,328 titles, of which GRID uses 97 references selected according to the values of deliberative democracy.

These references fall into two broad epistemological families. The first brings together Legislative Theatre: the canon, methods and cases linked to the Theatre of the Oppressed, facilitation, performative participation, conflict, embodied experience and the translation of collective experience into political action. The second brings together political and critical philosophy, postcolonial and decolonial theory, feminisms, critical pedagogy, theories of power, representation and epistemology.

The material is arranged on three "shelves": **Canon**, for theoretical and normative frameworks; **Methods**, for procedures and tools; and **Cases**, for situated experience and practical evidence. The canon is for interpreting; the methods, for moving from ideas to concrete devices; the cases, for checking what happened when those methods were put into practice.

Technically, GRID does not "read the whole corpus" or pick a reference because it is famous. Using semantic retrieval (RAG), it looks for the fragments conceptually closest to the question and builds its answer from them. The sequence is simple: question, search for relevant fragments, selection and grounded generation.

But this operation is never neutral. The corpus consulted, the categories used, the problems recognised and the solutions considered plausible all shape what GRID can bring into view. The same situation can be read as a problem of consensus, of institutional design, of structural inequality or of political conflict, depending on the framework it is seen through. The knowledge we build an agent with is not just content. It is also an infrastructure of interpretation.

### **From the heart of the beast to the *radical server***

There is one more layer of infrastructure we want to make visible: where the AI we use physically happens.

Platoniq works within a tradition of civic and technological experimentation. Our projects rarely start from a finished solution. They start from a question tested in a real situation: what tools does a community need to understand itself, decide and act? GRID belongs to that way of working. It is not a finished product or a technical showcase, but a research device that is built as it is used, observed, corrected and discussed with the people taking part.

That is why the first version of GRID was knowingly built in the heart of the beast: ChatGPT. This choice lets us work with highly capable, fast and constantly updated models, without having to build from scratch the enormous computing infrastructure behind them. It frees us to focus on what we actually want to experiment with: the corpus, the personalities, the retrieval rules, the traceability of sources and the relationship between artificial intelligence and deliberation.

But that convenience comes at a price: dependency. Even if we decide which documents GRID consults, how the corpus is organised or what instructions the agent receives, the engine that runs the inference remains outside our control. We do not decide how the base model was trained, what updates it receives or how it may change. We control part of GRID's epistemology, but not all of its infrastructure.

{% quote %}It is neither a finished product nor a demonstration of technical capabilities, but a research device that is built as it is used{% endquote %}

In parallel, we are prototyping a second architecture we call the ***radical server***. The idea is to take the experiment in the opposite direction: to shrink the infrastructure it needs, with the aim of running the system on everyday hardware under our own control. The challenge we have set ourselves is to get it running on the processor of a television. This is ongoing work, not a finished result.

The *radical server* explores whether, by narrowing the universe of knowledge and making explicit the rules by which it is mobilised, we can also reduce the computing power, energy and technological dependency required. It does not try to know everything. It tries to know where to look, to recognise the limits of what it holds and to work in depth with an archive selected and governed by the community that uses it.

This second path is also part of our method: prototype in real conditions, accept limitations and turn them into questions. A local model may be less capable, slower or wrong in different ways. But it opens up the possibility of deliberative infrastructures that do not send every conversation to a large data centre and that can run in community centres, schools, libraries or places with poor connectivity.

The tension between the two prototypes is, in itself, the experiment. It is not only about comparing performance, but about investigating something more political: what kind of artificial intelligence becomes possible when scale, infrastructure ownership and the limits of knowledge also become democratic decisions.

## **Call to action**

Perhaps the question is not whether AI can deliberate the way we do. The question is whether we can see, compare and collectively discuss how it is intervening in the way we deliberate.

We want to put GRID to the test in real deliberative settings. If you work in participation, facilitation, democratic research or AI governance, we would like to explore with you how to assess what each form of mediation reveals and conceals, what changes when different corpora or architectures are introduced, and where the line between assistance and human decision should lie.

Instead of building a single agent that aspires to know everything, we want to experiment with an ecology of partial, traceable and potentially contradictory agents: systems that can declare which corpus they speak from, show their differences and turn those differences into material for deliberation.

**Will you join us?**
